Start with a point. It has no length, no width, no thickness. It marks a place and nothing more. A point has zero dimensions: inside it, there is nowhere to go.

Every shape in this chapter is made with a single move. Take whatever you have and drag it in a new direction, one at right angles to every direction you have used so far.

The drawing changes as you read. The slider beneath it lets you do the dragging yourself.

## The fourth direction

There is no fourth direction in your room. It would have to be at right angles to <span class="axis-0">x</span>, <span class="axis-1">y</span> and <span class="axis-2">z</span> all at once. But the move doesn't need you to see the direction; it only needs the direction to exist, and mathematics is happy to grant that. Call it <span class="axis-3">w</span>, and draw it in violet.

  **Drag along <span class="axis-3">w</span>.** Drag the whole cube into <span class="axis-3">w</span>. Each of the eight corners sweeps a violet edge, and the copy slides away from you, shrinking as it goes.

  </Step>
  <Step>

  **A cube inside a cube.** This is what you saw when you looked straight down <span class="axis-2">z</span> at the cube. Then the copy moved away along <span class="axis-2">z</span>, so a small square sat inside a big one. Now it moves away along <span class="axis-3">w</span>, so a small cube sits inside a big one.

  The inner cube is really just as big as the outer one, and all eight violet edges are the same length. The six lopsided boxes between them are cubes too. Count them: one inside, one outside, six in between. Eight.

  </Step>
  <Step>

  **Turn it in <span class="axis-3">w</span>.** Move the xw slider to turn the tesseract in the plane of <span class="axis-0">x</span> and <span class="axis-3">w</span>. The inner cube swells and the outer one shrinks; halfway there, nothing is inside anything.

  After a quarter turn you have a cube inside a cube again, but a different pair, and now the red edges are the ones joining inside to outside. "Inside" was never a property of the tesseract, only of where you are looking from. Drag the drawing sideways to turn it as you would a cube.

  </Step>
</Scrolly>

<Deeper title="How many faces does a cube of any dimension have?">

Each $k$-dimensional face of the $n$-dimensional cube comes from two choices. First pick which $k$ of the $n$ directions the face spreads out along: there are $\binom{n}{k}$ ways. Along each of the remaining $n-k$ directions, the face has to sit at one end or the other: $2^{n-k}$ ways. So

$$
f(n,k) = \binom{n}{k}\,2^{\,n-k}.
$$

For the tesseract ($n=4$) that gives $4 \times 8 = 32$ edges, $6 \times 4 = 24$ squares and $4 \times 2 = 8$ cubes.

The table's pattern is hiding in here too. After one drag, every $k$-dimensional face either belongs to the original or the copy, or was swept out by a $(k-1)$-dimensional face as it moved:

$$
f(n,k) = 2\,f(n-1,k) + f(n-1,k-1).
$$

That is Pascal's triangle with a factor of 2 on one side.

</Deeper>

## Turning it around

<SquareSays>I followed every step and I could count every piece. I just cannot point to your z.</SquareSays>

What you feel about <span class="axis-3">w</span> is exactly what A Square feels about <span class="axis-2">z</span>: the rules are perfectly clear, and the direction is nowhere to be found. You can draw a tesseract because you flatten it into your own space, just as A Square could draw a cube in perspective on his paper.

Now turn the question around. All chapter long you stood outside, looking down as each shape was built. A Square has no "down" to look from. If something from Spaceland, a sphere say, passed through his plane, what would he see? How would he describe a thing he can never step back and look at?
