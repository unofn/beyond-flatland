In 1884 a London schoolmaster, Edwin Abbott, published a short book about a place called Flatland. Everything there lives on an endless sheet with no thickness: houses, trees, people, all flat figures. They slide forwards, backwards, left and right. There is no up and no down. Not forbidden: simply not there.

The book is narrated by a square. We will call him A Square.

## What A Square sees

You are looking down on a small patch of Flatland. A Square (in red) stands in the middle, with a pentagonal house and a few polygon neighbours around him.

Turn him all the way round and watch the strip.

That strip is what A Square actually sees. His eye lies in the sheet too, so every line of sight skims along the surface and stops at the first edge it meets. He never sees *into* anything. His whole view is a single line: a stretch of something here, a stretch of something there. The thick red strokes on the map are the edges he can see right now.

So how does he tell one neighbour from another? Abbott gave Flatland a permanent light fog. Distant things are faint, near things are distinct. The darker a part of the strip, the closer that edge is to him.

Turn him towards the triangle at the upper right and walk him a little closer. It points a corner straight at him, so the middle of its stretch is darkest and it fades on both sides: the tip is nearest and the two sides fall away. Now face a flat wall of the house. That stretch is shaded far more evenly. This is how Flatlanders read the world: from the way the fog grades across a stretch they judge corner or side, triangle or circle.

What you take in at a glance, he has to work out.

## The Sphere visits

One day A Square hears a voice. It says it comes from *above*.

  He keeps sinking, and the point opens into a circle that grows and grows. When his centre reaches the sheet the circle is as wide as the Sphere himself. In A Square's eye the speck has stretched into a line segment, wider and wider.

  </Step>
  <Step>

  Further down, the circle shrinks, closes to a point and is gone. The Sphere hasn't gone anywhere; he has only moved a little way *down*. From inside Flatland, something appeared by itself, grew, and vanished into thin air.

<SquareSays>A circle swelled and shrank in front of me and then was simply not there. He said he was many circles in one, and told me to look *upward*. I pointed north. I pointed south. I could not point to where he meant.</SquareSays>

The circle that appears in Flatland is the slice where the Sphere meets the sheet: a cross-section. There is one Sphere but endlessly many cross-sections, one for every height. A Square only ever sees one of them at a time.

<Deeper title="How big is the cross-section?">

Let the Sphere have radius $r$, with his centre a height $h$ above the sheet. The centre, the middle of the cross-section, and any point on its rim form a right triangle: the hypotenuse is $r$, one leg is $h$, and the other is the cross-section's radius $\rho$. By Pythagoras,

$$
\rho = \sqrt{r^2 - h^2}
$$

At $h = r$ we get $\rho = 0$, the point at first touch; at $h = 0$, $\rho = r$, the largest circle; for $|h| > r$ the square root has nothing to take, and Flatland is empty.

</Deeper>

## Other visitors

A sphere looks the same whichever way it arrives; it is too round to care. A cube is different. What Flatland sees depends on how it comes through.

<Plate caption="Switch the cube's orientation, then drag the slider or press play to pass it through Flatland." ratio="1">
  <CubeFigure client:only="react" locale="en" />
</Plate>

Choose *face first*. The instant the bottom face touches the sheet, a whole square appears in Flatland. It doesn't move or change size until the top face leaves, and then it is gone at once. A Square would simply think a new neighbour had arrived.

Now choose *corner first*. A point, then a triangle, growing; then its three corners are cut off and it becomes a hexagon, with all six sides equal at the exact middle; then it closes into a triangle turned the other way, and back to a point.

One cube, two quite different stories. A Flatlander who wanted to know what the visitor really was would have to record the slices one by one and stack them up in the mind.

## Now it's our turn

Now move everything up one dimension.

We live in Spaceland, with length, breadth and height, as much at home in it as A Square is on his sheet. Suppose a four-dimensional object passed through our space along a fourth direction. What would we see? Never the whole of it: only its cross-section. A solid that appears from nowhere, grows, changes shape and vanishes.
